Check before launch

  1. Ridgeline Nature Trail status. NPS’s South Unit page (updated May 2026) still says the trail is “currently inaccessible due to road collapse.” No closure is in the NPS alerts feed, and the script tells drivers to check the trailhead sign. Call the park (701-623-4466) to confirm.
  2. 35 mph speed limits. NPS says 25 mph unless posted. The 35 mph limits on the stem and the west side come from OpenStreetMap only. Confirm on the next drive; one line in app/data/tour.json changes them.
  3. Ranch-era details from Hagedorn. The bison-hunt victory dance, the $100, and Ferris’s “never saw any one so enthused” come from Hagedorn’s 1921 book, based on Ferris’s recollection. They are marked medium confidence.
  4. Wild horse origins. The script says the horses descend from ranch horses and horses from tribes in the area, fenced in when the park boundary went up in the 1950s (AP, 2024). Count of “about 200” is from NPS’s April 2026 update (198).
  5. The 1884 diary on display. The Feb. 14, 1884 story and the closing both say the diary is on display at the Library. Confirm the loan is current.
  6. Pronunciations. Listen to “de Morès” (Medora Overlook), “coteries” (Johnson’s Plateau) and “porcupette” (Little Missouri). Any line can be re-voiced alone.
  7. Joe Wiegand quote session. 17 Roosevelt quotations are read by Nora for now. Each one is tagged below; Joe’s recordings drop in without touching the rest.
  8. Library sightline at Skyline Vista. The on-foot clip tells people to look south toward Medora, then a little right of town, to find the Library on its butte. Confirm that direction standing at the overlook.
  9. Historical photos. Seventeen Houghton Library (Harvard) photos came from the DAM. Two are staged 1886 re-creations of the boat-thief capture and are captioned that way. Confirm the Harvard terms cover a free public web app.
  10. Artlist license. Confirm the plan covers AI voice-overs in a free public web app.
  11. Animal sounds. Bison, elk, coyote and prairie dog recordings are public-domain NPS recordings from other parks (Yellowstone, Wind Cave and others). The horse sounds are domestic horses. No free pronghorn recording exists. Artlist’s Yellowstone Sound Library has better bison takes if you want to swap them in.
  12. NPS’s own audio tour. NPS has an 11-stop Scenic Loop tour in the NPS App. This one is longer and story-driven; a heads-up to the park before launch is a good idea.

Stops, in road order

Mileage is clockwise from the visitor center (Peaceful Valley first). Counter-clockwise drivers hear the same clips in reverse order; nothing in the script assumes a direction.

South Unit Visitor Center. Mile 0.0 from the visitor center. Start. Plays when you park.

The Maltese Cross Cabin

  1. Nora

    Before you pull out — see the little log cabin behind the visitor center? That's the Maltese Cross Cabin. Roosevelt's first home out here.

  2. Wes

    It's not where it started, though.

  3. Nora

    Not even close. It stood about seven miles south of Medora on his first ranch. He'd bought into it in 1883, on his very first trip — about fourteen thousand dollars for cattle and a brand.

  4. Wes

    And the brand was a Maltese cross. Which the locals called the "maltee" cross.

  5. Nora

    Because they figured "maltese" had to be plural. Roosevelt was amused enough to put it in his autobiography.

  6. Wes

    [chuckles] Then the cabin went on tour.

  7. Nora

    It really did. The 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis. The 1905 exposition in Portland, Oregon. Then Fargo, and the state capitol grounds in Bismarck. It finally came here in 1959.

  8. Wes

    Look for the old graffiti on the door from all that traveling. And his trunk is inside — the real one, with "T.R." on it.

  9. Nora

    It's a two-minute walk. Worth it. We'll be right here when you get back.

Sources

AUTO ch. IV ("maltee cross"); NPS Maltese Cross Cabin page ($14,000; seven miles south of Medora; 1904 St. Louis, 1905 Portland, Bismarck, park 1959; graffiti; trunk)

Medora Overlook. Mile 0.3 from the visitor center. Plays as you pass.

Medora, and a Frenchman with big plans

  1. Nora

    That's Medora down there, along the river.

  2. Wes

    Which, when Roosevelt first got off the train, wasn't really a town yet.

  3. Nora

    Right. The train stopped at a place called Little Missouri, on the other bank. A station and a ramshackle hotel. Medora was brand new — a Frenchman, the Marquis de Morès, had just started it.

  4. Wes

    Named for his wife, Medora.

  5. Nora

    Roosevelt even put that in the preface of his first ranch book — "for the benefit of the future local historian."

  6. Wes

    [laughs] Hi. That's us.

  7. Nora

    The Marquis built a meatpacking plant. The idea was to slaughter cattle right here and ship the beef east in refrigerated rail cars.

  8. Wes

    Bold idea. Didn't work. Within about three years it had collapsed. But the brick chimney's still standing in town. Look for it when you're back in Medora.

Sources

AUTO ch. IV; HTR preface (Medora named for the Marquis's wife); Medora history doc (packing plant 1883, collapse 1886, chimney); RL ch. VIII

  7. Nora

  8. Wes

Skyline Vista. Mile 4.1 from the visitor center. Pull-off.

Skyline Vista — where it all started

  1. Nora

    Skyline Vista's coming up. This is a good first stop — a short, paved walk out to the edge, and the whole river valley opens up.

  2. Wes

    Here's a fun one for the walk. Roosevelt's first night out here.

  3. Nora

    September of 1883. Northern Pacific train pulls in around three in the morning. He's twenty-four. He came to hunt a bison before they were all gone.

  4. Wes

    And the only building besides the station is a place called the Pyramid Park Hotel.

  5. Nora

    Which he described as "ramshackle." He slept in a room with fourteen beds.

  6. Wes

    Fourteen. That's not a hotel room. That's a bunkhouse with a sign.

  7. Nora

    [laughs] And yet he kept coming back for years.

Sources

AUTO ch. IV (arrival, Pyramid Park Hotel, fourteen beds); NPS Skyline Vista (paved, accessible, short)

Skyline Vista. Mile 4.1 from the visitor center. Pull-off. Plays when you park.

On foot at Skyline Vista

  1. Wes

    Nice, you stopped. The trail's flat and paved — just a few minutes out and back.

  2. Nora

    That's the river valley he first rode into. And if you need a signal for anything, this is one of the few spots out here that usually has one.

  3. Wes

    Oh, and one more thing to find from out there. Look south, back toward Medora, and then a little to the right of town, up on the butte.

  4. Nora

    See the long, low building with grass growing on the roof? That's the Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library.

  5. Wes

    It's easy to miss. And that's on purpose.

  6. Nora

    That ridge can be seen from inside the park, so everything up there was kept low. The architects — Snøhetta, the Norwegian firm behind the opera house in Oslo — tucked the building into the butte instead of setting it on top.

  7. Wes

    And the roof is real prairie. About a hundred and forty thousand native plants. You can walk right up onto it.

  8. Nora

    The walls by the front door are rammed earth. Local soil, packed down in layers, so they're striped just like these buttes.

  9. Wes

    Inside, it's his whole life — the kid from New York, the rancher out here, the president, the conservationist. Outside, the Boardwalk loops out across restored prairie.

  10. Nora

    And from up on that roof, you can look right back across the valley to where you're standing now.

Sources

NPS Skyline Vista; NPS cell coverage; TRPL Press Backgrounder §6.2 (Snøhetta, sited into the butte, 140,000 native plants on the roof, rammed earth from local soil, walkable roof with views of the park); TRPL board deck (ridge visible from the park → height limits); TRPL Story Guide (exhibits, Boardwalk)

Little Missouri River. Mile 5.2 from the visitor center. Plays as you pass.

The Little Missouri

  1. Nora

    Down to your left, that line of big trees — that's the Little Missouri River. Those are cottonwoods.

  2. Wes

    The river's the only water in the park with fish in it. Some summers it's barely a trickle. Spring, it can flood.

  3. Nora

    And in winter it freezes solid. Roosevelt had a great line about that, from his ranch.

  4. Quote

    The river lay in its bed fixed and immovable as a bar of bent steel, and then at night wolves and lynxes traveled up and down it as if it had been a highway passing in front of the ranch house. AUTO ch. IV, 1913

  5. Wes

    No wolves now. But here's a tip — look up in those cottonwoods. Way up. Sometimes there's a dark lump in the branches.

  6. Nora

    Porcupine.

  7. Wes

    Porcupine. Easiest in fall and winter when the leaves are down. A baby is called a porcupette.

  8. Nora

    You just like saying porcupette.

  9. Wes

    Everybody likes saying porcupette.

Sources 1 TR quote for Joe

NPS rivers page; NPS forests/floodplains; NPS porcupine page; AUTO ch. IV (quote "bar of bent steel")

Peaceful Valley Ranch. Mile 6.8 clockwise, 28.0 counter-clockwise. Pull-off.

Peaceful Valley Ranch

  1. Nora

    Peaceful Valley Ranch is up here. Restrooms, too, if anybody's asking.

  2. Wes

    Somebody's always asking.

  3. Nora

    This one's not a Roosevelt place, but it's a great story. It's the only original ranch house left in this part of the park — built around 1885.

  4. Wes

    And in 1918 the family who owned it started one of the first dude ranches around.

  5. Nora

    Easterners paying to come out and play cowboy. Which, honestly, is a little bit what Roosevelt did.

  6. Wes

    [laughs] Don't let him hear you say that.

  7. Nora

    Later the government bought it. In the 1930s it housed Depression-era work crews, and then it was the park's first headquarters, until the visitor center in Medora opened in 1959.

  8. Wes

    They ran trail rides out of here until 2014. No rides now, but the buildings are still here, and the cottonwoods are huge.

Sources

NPS Peaceful Valley Ranch page (Paddock 1883; Lamb house c.1885; Olsens 1918 dude ranch; named 1922; govt 1936; RDA HQ; park HQ 1947–1959; rides ended 2014; no TR connection)

Beef Corral Bottom. Mile 8.8 clockwise, 26.0 counter-clockwise. Plays as you pass.

Beef Corral Bottom

  1. Wes

    Another prairie dog town coming up — Beef Corral Bottom. Scan the far edges of this one.

  2. Nora

    For what?

  3. Wes

    Pronghorn. Tan and white, with a black face mask. They only wander into the park now and then, but this is one of their spots.

  4. Nora

    They're the fast ones.

  5. Wes

    Fastest land animal in North America. Up to fifty-five miles an hour. Second fastest on the planet, behind the cheetah.

  6. Nora

    And they're not actually antelope, right?

  7. Wes

    Nope. They're the last surviving species in their whole family. And they're the only animal that sheds the outer part of its horns every year.

  8. Nora

    You're going to be insufferable today.

  9. Wes

    I've been insufferable for years.

Sources

NPS wildlife viewing (pronghorn at Beef Corral; enter park only occasionally); NPS pronghorn page (55 mph; second-fastest; 320°; shed horn sheaths; not true antelope)

Wind Canyon

  1. Nora

Jones Creek. Mile 14.4 clockwise, 20.5 counter-clockwise. Plays as you pass.

The boys who built the park

  1. Nora

    This area along Jones Creek — there used to be a camp near here. Young men from the Civilian Conservation Corps.

  2. Wes

    The Depression program. Put young guys to work on public lands.

  3. Nora

    Three companies worked in these badlands in the 1930s, about two hundred men each. One camped near Jones Creek until late 1941.

  4. Wes

    Pay was thirty dollars a month, and most of it got sent home to their families.

  5. Nora

    They built parts of the roads and trails we're on today. They cut sandstone by hand. One of them said later —

  6. Nora

    "You learned how to live with other men, you learned self esteem… you learned about yourself."

  7. Wes

    That's a good line.

  8. Nora

    They also built the old stone entrance station on the south side of the loop. It's still standing.

Sources

Buck Hill. End of side road. Side road. Plays when you park.

The top of Buck Hill

  1. Wes

    Okay. Look down at the rock at the top. See the little ridges in it? Like the sand at the bottom of a creek?

  2. Nora

    Oh, those are ripples.

  3. Wes

    Ripple marks. This hilltop — the highest point around — used to be the bottom of a stream.

  4. Nora

    That's wild.

  5. Wes

    The land here was a flat, swampy delta. Rivers coming off the young Rocky Mountains dumped layer after layer of mud and sand. Then a river started cutting down through it all, and here we are, standing on the old streambed.

  6. Nora

    Come back on a clear night, too. No big city for over thirty miles. You can see the Milky Way. Sometimes even the northern lights.

Coal Vein turnoff. Mile 19.6 clockwise, 15.2 counter-clockwise. Side-road turn.

The Coal Vein

  1. Nora

    Here's the turn for the Coal Vein. It's a short gravel road to a trailhead.

  2. Wes

    Okay, quick story to decide if you want to go. In 1951, a seam of coal underground here caught fire. Probably lightning.

  3. Nora

    And it burned for twenty-six years.

  4. Wes

    Twenty-six years! People came to watch the smoke and the glowing coals. Some of them roasted marshmallows over it.

  5. Nora

    Over a coal fire in a national park.

  6. Wes

    It was a different time. The trail walks you right through where the ground collapsed as the coal burned away.

Coal Vein Trail. End of side road. Side road. Plays when you park.

On foot at the Coal Vein

  1. Wes

    The trail's a loop with numbered posts and some stairs. Stay left and follow the posts.

  2. Nora

    The guide says before the fire, the ground was level with the top of the stairs.

  3. Wes

    So everything below that just — burned and sank.

  4. Nora

    Look for two other things. The red rock is coal-fire rock — baked clay. Geologists call it clinker. Folks around here call it scoria.

  5. Wes

    And the gray stuff that looks like popcorn? That's bentonite. Old volcanic ash. When it gets wet, it can soak up five times its weight in water and turns into slick, sticky mud.

  6. Nora

    Which is why whole hillsides out here slowly slide.

  7. Wes

    Roosevelt had a coal vein on his ranch, too. They dug it out of the hillside to heat the house.

The Old East Entrance

  1. Nora

  8. Wes

Badlands Overlook. Mile 23.8 clockwise, 11.1 counter-clockwise. Pull-off.

Reading the layers

  1. Wes

    Badlands Overlook is up here. Great place to read the rock layers like a book.

  2. Nora

    Okay, teach me.

  3. Wes

    Lower down, the brighter yellow and tan layers — that's one formation. Higher up, the grayer, browner stuff is another. All of it is mud, sand and volcanic ash that washed east off the Rocky Mountains, somewhere around fifty-five to sixty million years ago.

  4. Nora

    And the dark stripes?

  5. Wes

    Coal. Old swamp plants, squeezed down over millions of years.

  6. Nora

    So why is it all carved up like this?

  7. Wes

    Glaciers. The Little Missouri used to flow north, toward Hudson Bay. Ice sheets blocked it and pushed it east. The new route was steeper, so the river started cutting down fast — and it's been carving these badlands ever since.

Sources

NDGS Hoganson & Campbell (Bullion Creek yellow/tan, Sentinel Butte gray/brown; 55–60 Ma); NPS geologic formations (Rockies sediment, swamp, bentonite); NPS glaciers/Painted Canyon (river diverted)

Scoria Point Overlook. Mile 25.7 clockwise, 9.1 counter-clockwise. Pull-off.

Scoria Point

  1. Nora

    Scoria Point's next. See the red caps on top of the buttes?

  2. Wes

    That's coal-fire rock. Geologists call it clinker. When a coal seam burns underground, it bakes the clay above it into a natural brick. It's harder than the rock around it, so it sits on top like a helmet while everything below erodes away.

  3. Nora

    Roosevelt noticed it.

  4. Quote

    When a coal vein gets on fire it makes what is called a burning mine, and the clay above it is turned into brick; so that where water wears away the side of a hill sharp streaks of black and red are seen across it, mingled with the grays, purples, and browns. HTR ch. I, 1885

  5. Wes

    Nailed it. Although — he also thought some of these buttes were volcanic.

  6. Nora

    They're not.

  7. Wes

    They're not. Real scoria is volcanic rock. The name just stuck. Watch for bison on these slopes, too.

Sources 1 TR quote for Joe

NPS Scoria Point; NDGS Bluemle "North Dakota's Clinker"; HTR ch. I (quote 6 excerpt; TR thought volcanic)

Prairie Dog Town. Mile 28.3 clockwise, 6.6 counter-clockwise. Pull-off.

The prairie dog town by the junction

  1. Wes

    Big prairie dog town right here, with a pull-off and a sidewalk. If you've got two minutes, this is the one to stop at.

  2. Nora

    Listen for the alarms. Their barks sound more like squeaks. And they have different calls for different threats — a hawk, a coyote, a person.

  3. Wes

    Then there's my favorite. The jump-yip. One prairie dog stretches straight up, throws its arms in the air, and yips —

  4. Nora

    — and then the next one does it, and the next one —

  5. Wes

    — like a stadium wave! Scientists think it's how they check whether the neighbors are paying attention.

  6. Sound

    Prairie Dog Yip (Wind Cave NP) (Sound: National Park Service (public domain))

  7. Nora

    Here's a number. The Park Service says that in 1901, scientists surveyed one prairie dog town that covered twenty-five thousand square miles. An estimated four hundred million prairie dogs.

  8. Wes

    One town.

  9. Nora

    One town. Poisoning campaigns wiped most of them out.

  10. Wes

    And please don't feed them. They bite, human food makes them sick, and their fleas can carry plague. Just watch.

Sources

NPS prairie dogs page (alarm calls, "all clear", plague fleas, don't feed, 1901 survey); Hare et al. 2014 (jump-yip); NPS (biologists' "keystone" usage attributed)

Plays once on the rebuilt south stretch, either direction

The road that slid away

  1. Nora

    Fun fact about this stretch of road. For years, you couldn't drive it.

  2. Wes

    In 2019, heavy rain soaked the hillsides and sections of road just — slid down the slope.

  3. Nora

    Why does that keep happening out here?

  4. Wes

    A big part of it is bentonite. It's old volcanic ash in the rock. Soak it and it turns slick, and whole hillsides slowly slide. Engineers found more unstable ground, and you couldn't drive the full loop for six years. The fix ran more than six miles and cost about fifty-one million dollars.

  5. Nora

    One senator said it was almost like building a bridge.

  6. Wes

    It reopened in late 2025, in the middle of the first big snow of the winter. So if anybody who visited a few years ago tells you the loop's closed — not anymore.

Sources

NPS Nov 25 2025 release (2019 slides; 4-mile stretch; two more miles unsafe; >6 miles; ~$51M; GAOA); ND Monitor (Hoeven "almost like building a bridge")

The wild horses

  1. Wes

    Let's talk horses. You might see some today — they're all over this part of the park.

  2. Nora

    Where did they even come from?

  3. Wes

    Historians trace them to ranch horses, and horses from the tribes in the area. When the park fenced its boundary back in the 1950s, some of them ended up inside.

  4. Nora

    And they've been here ever since.

  5. Wes

    Pretty much. Families of them, with a lead stallion, living out here on their own. The Park Service manages them as a cultural resource — part of the history of the place.

  6. Nora

    There was a real fight over them a few years ago.

  7. Wes

    There was. The park looked at removing them, and people spoke up — a lot of people. In 2024 the Park Service committed to keeping a herd here.

  8. Nora

    How many are there?

  9. Wes

    About two hundred. One researcher called them "living history." I like that.

  10. Sound

    Horse whinnies, Supplementary Audio S1 (Briefer et al. 2015, Sci. Rep. 5:9989) (Briefer E. et al. (2015) "Segregation of information about emotional arousal and valence in horse whinnies", Scientific Reports, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons)
