# Scenic Loop with Nora & Wes — tour script

Format: each `# CLIP id` block is one audio clip. `N:` = Nora (voice: Resonance), `W:` = Wes (voice: Keen).
`Q:` = a verbatim Theodore Roosevelt quote, read by Nora for now (marked for Joe Wiegand to re-record). Source in {braces}.
`SFX:` = a real animal recording dropped into the clip. `TRV:` = Roosevelt's own recorded voice.
`src:` lines are research citations for fact-checking (keys in research/facts.md). They are not read aloud.
Audio tags in [brackets] (e.g. [laughs]) are performance cues for the voice model.

---

# CLIP welcome
title: Welcome to the Badlands
kind: start
src: AUTO ch. IV (arrival); NPS South Unit page (36 miles, 2 hours); NPS wildlife viewing (25 yards)
N: Hey, welcome! I'm Nora.
W: And I'm Wes. We're riding along with you today, all the way around the Scenic Loop.
N: Thirty-six miles of the North Dakota Badlands. Buttes, prairie dog towns, bison, wild horses —
W: — and a guy from New York City who showed up here at three in the morning and never really got over it.
N: [laughs] That's the short version of Theodore Roosevelt, yes.
W: Quick ground rules before we roll. Mount the phone where you can glance at it, but don't drive by it. The screen does its thing on its own while you're moving. When you park, it opens up — photos, maps, little games.
N: We talk when there's something worth talking about. You just drive. GPS tells us where you are, so the stories show up right where they belong.
W: And we're timed for the speed limit. Go too fast and you'll drive right past half the good stuff. The bison aren't in a hurry. Neither are we.
N: One more. Out here, every animal gets at least twenty-five yards. That's about two school buses. More is better. If an animal moves away from you, you're too close.
W: Okay. Windows down if the weather's nice. Let's go find some bison.

kind: stop-parked
stop: visitor-center
src: AUTO ch. IV ("maltee cross"); NPS Maltese Cross Cabin page ($14,000; seven miles south of Medora; 1904 St. Louis, 1905 Portland, Bismarck, park 1959; graffiti; trunk)
N: Before you pull out — see the little log cabin behind the visitor center? That's the Maltese Cross Cabin. Roosevelt's first home out here.
W: It's not where it started, though.
N: Not even close. It stood about seven miles south of Medora on his first ranch. He'd bought into it in 1883, on his very first trip — about fourteen thousand dollars for cattle and a brand.
W: And the brand was a Maltese cross. Which the locals called the "maltee" cross.
N: Because they figured "maltese" had to be plural. Roosevelt was amused enough to put it in his autobiography.
W: [chuckles] Then the cabin went on tour.
N: It really did. The 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis. The 1905 exposition in Portland, Oregon. Then Fargo, and the state capitol grounds in Bismarck. It finally came here in 1959.
W: Look for the old graffiti on the door from all that traveling. And his trunk is inside — the real one, with "T.R." on it.
N: It's a two-minute walk. Worth it. We'll be right here when you get back.

# CLIP badlands-overlook
title: Reading the layers
kind: stop-approach
stop: badlands-overlook
src: NDGS Hoganson & Campbell (Bullion Creek yellow/tan, Sentinel Butte gray/brown; 55–60 Ma); NPS geologic formations (Rockies sediment, swamp, bentonite); NPS glaciers/Painted Canyon (river diverted)
W: Badlands Overlook is up here. Great place to read the rock layers like a book.
N: Okay, teach me.
W: Lower down, the brighter yellow and tan layers — that's one formation. Higher up, the grayer, browner stuff is another. All of it is mud, sand and volcanic ash that washed east off the Rocky Mountains, somewhere around fifty-five to sixty million years ago.
N: And the dark stripes?
W: Coal. Old swamp plants, squeezed down over millions of years.
N: So why is it all carved up like this?
W: Glaciers. The Little Missouri used to flow north, toward Hudson Bay. Ice sheets blocked it and pushed it east. The new route was steeper, so the river started cutting down fast — and it's been carving these badlands ever since.

# CLIP rebuilt-road
title: The road that slid away
kind: story
zone: rebuilt-section
src: NPS Nov 25 2025 release (2019 slides; 4-mile stretch; two more miles unsafe; >6 miles; ~$51M; GAOA); ND Monitor (Hoeven "almost like building a bridge")
N: Fun fact about this stretch of road. For years, you couldn't drive it.
W: In 2019, heavy rain soaked the hillsides and sections of road just — slid down the slope.
N: Why does that keep happening out here?
W: A big part of it is bentonite. It's old volcanic ash in the rock. Soak it and it turns slick, and whole hillsides slowly slide. Engineers found more unstable ground, and you couldn't drive the full loop for six years. The fix ran more than six miles and cost about fifty-one million dollars.
N: One senator said it was almost like building a bridge.
W: It reopened in late 2025, in the middle of the first big snow of the winter. So if anybody who visited a few years ago tells you the loop's closed — not anymore.

# CLIP scoria-point
title: Scoria Point
kind: stop-approach
stop: scoria-point
src: NPS Scoria Point; NDGS Bluemle "North Dakota's Clinker"; HTR ch. I (quote 6 excerpt; TR thought volcanic)
N: Scoria Point's next. See the red caps on top of the buttes?
W: That's coal-fire rock. Geologists call it clinker. When a coal seam burns underground, it bakes the clay above it into a natural brick. It's harder than the rock around it, so it sits on top like a helmet while everything below erodes away.
N: Roosevelt noticed it.
Q: When a coal vein gets on fire it makes what is called a burning mine, and the clay above it is turned into brick; so that where water wears away the side of a hill sharp streaks of black and red are seen across it, mingled with the grays, purples, and browns. {HTR ch. I, 1885}
W: Nailed it. Although — he also thought some of these buttes were volcanic.
N: They're not.
W: They're not. Real scoria is volcanic rock. The name just stuck. Watch for bison on these slopes, too.
