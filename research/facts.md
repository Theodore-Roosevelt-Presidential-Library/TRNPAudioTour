# Fact Sheet: South Unit Scenic Loop Audio Tour
Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library. Prepared 2026-10-10 for the scriptwriter.

**How to use this sheet**
- Every fact carries a source key and a confidence tag. **HIGH** means a primary source or an authoritative agency page, checked directly. **MED** means one good secondary source, a reported (not written) TR remark, or an agency figure that other sources contradict.
- Where two sources disagree, both are recorded under **CONFLICT**. Do not resolve a conflict in the script; use the safe wording given.
- All TR quotations in Section H were copied from the scanned or transcribed book text. Do not tidy the spelling ("Bad Lands," "cotton-woods," "Tolstoi").

## A. TR in the Dakota Badlands

### Facts

1. **Arrival.** TR "first reached the Little Missouri on a Northern Pacific train about three in the morning of a cool September day in 1883." The only building besides the station was "a ramshackle structure called the Pyramid Park Hotel." He slept in a room with fourteen beds. AUTO ch. IV. **HIGH**
   - **Date.** TRC (Joe Ferris entry) and the TRPL Press Backgrounder give **September 8, 1883**. Because he arrived at 3 a.m., some writers give the night of Sept. 7–8. PRJ-MED. **MED**
   - **CONFLICT:** The Press Backgrounder says "at 2 in the morning." TR himself says "about three." Use TR's wording.
   - The station was "Little Missouri," on the west bank. Medora, on the east bank, was the Marquis's new town (see B). The TRPL Story Guide says trains began stopping in Medora in 1884. **HIGH** (AUTO; Story Guide p. 43)
2. **The bison hunt.** Joe Ferris guided him. Days of cold rain followed. They found the bull near Gregor Lang's cabin on the Little Cannonball. AUTO ("After a buffalo hunt with my original friend, Joe Ferris"); HAG ch. II–III. **HIGH**
   - **CONFLICT on length:** NPS says a "15-day hunting trip" (https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/elkhorn-ranch.htm); TRC says "ten-day hunt." Don't give a number.
3. **Buying in.** Before he left in 1883, he went into partnership with Sylvane Ferris and William J. Merrifield on the Chimney Butte ranch, "with the maltese cross brand--always known as 'maltee cross,' by the way, as the general impression along the Little Missouri was that 'maltese' must be a plural." AUTO. **HIGH**
   - NPS: he "invested $14,000 for cattle and a brand," and the ranch was "seven miles south of Medora." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/maltese-cross-cabin.htm **HIGH** (NPS figure)
4. **February 14, 1884.** His mother, Martha "Mittie" Bulloch Roosevelt, died of typhoid fever early that morning. His wife, Alice Hathaway Lee Roosevelt, died that afternoon, two days after their daughter Alice was born. Both died in the same house in New York. TR's diary for that day: a large X and "The light has gone out of my life." TRC o284449 (LOC Manuscript Division); PRJ-MED. **HIGH**
   - **Tie-in:** that 1884 diary is on loan from the Library of Congress and on display at TRPL (TRPL Press Backgrounder). **HIGH** (internal source)
   - Do not use HAG for the date of death. HAG ch. IV puts both deaths on the wrong day (PRJ-MED).
5. **Return.** His diary for June 9, 1884 reads: "Arrived at my cattle ranche (Chimney Butte Ranche) on the Little Missouri." TRC o284449. **HIGH**
6. **Elkhorn.** In 1884 he chose a second site "about 35 miles north of Medora (per NPS)" and paid the previous occupant $400 for the rights. NPS-EK. **HIGH**
   - TR: "I brought out from Maine, in order to start the Elkhorn ranch lower down the river, my two backwoods friends Sewall and Dow. My brands for the lower ranch were the elkhorn and triangle." AUTO. **HIGH**
   - **CONFLICT on distance:** TR's own 1885 brand notice (in HAG ch. XI) says "twenty-five miles north of railroad," probably as the crow flies. Use NPS's 35 and don't mix the two.
7. **Building the Elkhorn.** Bill Sewall and his nephew Wilmot Dow cut cottonwood logs from mid-October 1884 and finished by spring 1885. The house was 30 × 60 ft with 7-ft walls, eight rooms, and a piazza on the east side. NPS-EK; TRC Sewall entry via PRJ-MED. **HIGH**
   - In 1886 the wives of Sewall and Dow both gave birth to sons at the ranch. NPS-EK. **HIGH**
8. **The boat thieves, March–April 1886.** TR's diary (quoted in HAG ch. XXII) gives the timeline:
   - March 24: "Thieves stole boat; started to build another to go after them."
   - March 30: TR, Sewall and Dow set off downriver in a homemade scow.
   - April 1: "Captured the three boat-thieves." It was the third day out.
   - April 2–6: stuck behind ice jams.
   - April 7: reached the C Diamond ranch.
   - He then hired a wagon and walked the prisoners overland to Dickinson. It "took us two days and a night," and he stayed awake about 36 hours.
   - Sources: HAG [diary]; RL ch. VIII; PRJ-MED. **HIGH**
   - **Who.** The leader was "Redhead" Mike Finnegan (RL spells it "Finnigan"). TRC's complaint record gives the other two as Edward Burnstead and Chris Pfaffenbach. PRJ-MED. **HIGH**
   - **Pay.** TR was a Billings County deputy sheriff. He collected fees plus "mileage for the three hundred odd miles gone over — a total of some fifty dollars." RL ch. VIII. **HIGH**
     - The 300 miles is the whole trip. Don't say he walked 300 miles.
   - **Outcome.** Finnegan and Burnstad (spelling varies) got 25 months in the Bismarck penitentiary; charges against Pfaffenbach were dropped. PRJ-MED (Prairie Public). **MED**
9. **Stockmen.** The Little Missouri River Stockmen's Association first met in Medora on Dec. 19, 1884, and elected TR chairman. TRC o280082; St. Paul Pioneer Press, Dec. 23, 1884. **HIGH**
   - He was also "the delegate to it from the Little Missouri" at the Stockman's Association of Montana meetings in Miles City. AUTO. **HIGH**
10. **Winter of 1886–87.** TR wrote that the storms began "About the middle of November" and that "By January the drifts had filled the ravines and coulees almost level." RL ch. V. **HIGH**
    - NPS: he "lost approximately 60% of his cattle." NPS-EK. **MED** (attribute it to NPS)
    - Letter to his sister Corinne: "I am bluer than indigo about the cattle." HAG ch. XXVI. **HIGH**
    - **Do not use:** the project guide's "nearly 80 percent of the region's cattle." No source was found for it.
11. **Winding down.** After an 1890 visit he told Sewall, "This is the last year I shall keep the ranch house open." He sold the Elkhorn to Sylvane Ferris in 1898. By 1901, "every scrap of the Elkhorn Ranch had disappeared with the exception of a couple of half rotted foundations" (a local stockman, quoted by NPS). NPS-EK. **HIGH**
12. **Boone and Crockett Club.** Proposed at a dinner at TR's New York home in December 1887. TR and George Bird Grinnell were the founders and TR was the first president. The aims included preserving native big game. Wikipedia, "Boone and Crockett Club" (infobox gives Dec. 21, 1887). **MED**; confirm with boone-crockett.org before giving an exact day.
13. **Conservation record.** NPS: "150 national forests, 51 federal bird reserves, 4 national game preserves, 5 national parks, and 18 national monuments … approximately 230 million acres of public land." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/theodore-roosevelt-and-conservation.htm. Palmer's 1912 USDA list independently confirms 51 bird reservations. PRJ-CON. **HIGH**
    - The link from the Badlands to his conservation is interpretive. Brinkley (*Wilderness Warrior*, 2009) argues it. Present it as interpretation, not fact.
14. **Maltese Cross Cabin**
    - A one-and-a-half-story ponderosa pine cabin with a shingled roof, root cellar and three rooms. NPS-MC. **HIGH**
    - NPS: "The logs … are the original ponderosa pine used in 1883." The safe date is "built 1883–84." PRJ-MED. **HIGH**
    - Its travels:
      - The 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis
      - The Lewis and Clark Centennial Exposition in Portland, Oregon (1905)
      - Fargo
      - The State Capitol grounds in Bismarck, cared for by the DAR
      - Theodore Roosevelt National Park in 1959. It now stands behind the South Unit Visitor Center.
      - Sources: NPS-MC; HABS ND-1. **HIGH** (Portland year: NPS plus Wikipedia)
    - Graffiti from its travels still marks the door. NPS-MC. **HIGH**
    - The wicker-and-duck-skin "T.R." trunk is his. The writing desk is a replica; the original is in the park museum. NPS-MC. **HIGH**
    - **Caution:** the cabin never stood in Medora.

### Anecdotes

- **"The boss he beavered down seventeen."** While cutting cottonwoods for the Elkhorn, someone asked Dow the tally. Not knowing TR could hear, he said: "Well, Bill cut down fifty-three, I cut forty-nine, and the boss he beavered down seventeen." TR added: "Those who have seen the stump of a tree which has been gnawed down by a beaver will understand the exact force of the comparison." AUTO. **HIGH**
- **The buffalo.** After a week of rain, Joe Ferris whispered where to aim. When the bull was down, Ferris called it dead "as Methusalem's cat." Then TR danced around the carcass and handed Ferris $100. HAG ch. III. **MED**; this is Hagedorn's narrative and Ferris's later recollection.
  - Ferris: "I never saw any one so enthused in my life."
  - **Wording caution:** Hagedorn calls the dance an "Indian war-dance." Paraphrase it as a "victory dance."
- **Anna Karenina on the ice.** Guarding the boat thieves: "As for me, I had brought with me 'Anna Karenina,' and my surroundings were quite gray enough to harmonize well with Tolstoi." RL ch. VIII. **HIGH**
  - Finnegan later told him: "If I'd had any show at all, you'd have sure had to fight, Mr. Roosevelt; but there wasn't any use making a break when I'd only have got shot myself." RL ch. VIII, as TR reports it. **HIGH**

## B. Medora and the Marquis de Morès

1. **Founding.** Antoine-Amédée-Marie-Vincent Manca de Vallombrosa, Marquis de Morès, born in Paris on June 14, 1858, founded Medora on the east bank of the Little Missouri, opposite the existing Little Missouri station. Project doc `The_History_of_Medora_North_Dakota.md`; HAG ch. IV. **HIGH**
   - HAG: "On April Fool's Day, 1883--auspicious date!--he pitched his tent in the sagebrush and founded the town of Medora." **MED** (HAG only; other sources say "April 1883")
2. **The name.** He named it for his wife, Medora. TR wrote: "the little hamlet of Medora — so named, I may mention for the benefit of the future local historian, in honor of the wife of the Marquis de Mores." HTR preface. **HIGH**
   - Her maiden name, von Hoffman, comes from the TRPL Story Guide and SHSND ("the wealthy von Hoffmans from New York"). **MED**; the exact form "Medora von Hoffman" was not checked against a primary record.
3. **Packing plant.**
   - The Northern Pacific Refrigerator Car Co. was incorporated May 12, 1883. The plant opened in the first week of October 1883 at about 15 head a day.
   - An enlarged 1885 plant could ship 150 cattle a day.
   - The scheme collapsed by fall 1886.
   - The plant burned in 1907; its brick chimney still stands in Chimney Park.
   - Sources: project Medora history doc; SHSND via PRJ-MED. **MED-HIGH**
   - Why it failed:
     - Range cattle were fat only in autumn.
     - Buyers preferred corn-fed beef.
     - Rebates were alleged to favor Chicago packers.
     - Source: project Medora history doc. **MED**
4. **The Chateau de Morès.** A 26-room, two-story frame summer home built in 1883. It is now an SHSND State Historic Site with many original furnishings. SHSND via PRJ-MED. **HIGH**
5. **Boomtown.** By the end of 1884, Medora had 251 permanent residents plus 50–100 transients. Project Medora history doc; TRPL Press Backgrounder. **MED** (both internal)
6. **TR and the Marquis.**
   - They dined together, but relations were tense.
   - In 1885 de Morès sent a letter TR read as a possible challenge. Quoted by HAG ch. XXI, TR said "I expect he'll challenge me." No duel happened.
   - Sources: HAG; project Medora history doc. **MED**
   - PRJ-MED advises leaving the challenge story and the 1883 Luffsey killing out of public copy because the details are contested.
7. **Hard frontier town.** TR in 1888: "Medora, in particular, has had more than its full share of shooting and stabbing affrays, horse-stealing, and cattle-killing." RL ch. VIII. **HIGH**
   - He also called it "the forlorn little cattle town which was our county seat." AUTO. **HIGH**
8. **Later.** De Morès left for France and was killed in North Africa (the Sahara) in 1896. TRPL Press Backgrounder. **MED**
9. **Rough Riders Hotel.** Built in 1884 as the Metropolitan Hotel. Story Guide; HAG. **HIGH**
   - **CONFLICT on the rename year:** SAH Archipedia says 1903; the TRPL Story Guide says 1905. Say "renamed after the Spanish-American War."
   - **Don't** say TR stayed there or gave a balcony speech; both are unsupported (PRJ-MED).

## C. Park history

1. **The memorial idea.** The 1921 North Dakota legislature asked Congress to set land aside for a park. The 1924–25 tours, including a "cowboys and Congressmen" trip, built support. https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/park-history.htm. **HIGH**
   - In 1928, NPS's Roger Toll wrote: "A national park does not seem to be justified." Same page. **HIGH** (good irony line)
2. **Depression era.**
   - 1934: a cooperative "Roosevelt Regional Park Project" began, with the CCC run by NPS staff.
   - 1935: designated the Roosevelt Recreation Demonstration Area (RDA).
   - 1941: work ended.
   - November 1946: the RDA was transferred to USFWS as Theodore Roosevelt National Wildlife Refuge.
   - Source: NPS park history. **HIGH**
3. **Memorial park.** On April 25, 1947, President Truman signed P.L. 80-38 creating Theodore Roosevelt National Memorial Park, covering roughly today's South Unit plus the Elkhorn site. Rep. William Lemke championed it. Sources: NPS; 61 Stat. 52 (PRJ-CON). **HIGH**
   - North Unit added June 12, 1948. Dedication June 4, 1949. **HIGH**
   - "As a memorial park, it was the only one of its kind in the National Park System." NPS. **HIGH**
4. **National park.** On November 10, 1978, President Carter signed P.L. 95-625, making it Theodore Roosevelt National Park and designating 29,920 acres of wilderness. NPS; 92 Stat. 3467. **HIGH**
   - **CONFLICT on acreage:** NPS park history says 70,448; the NPS Geodiversity Atlas says 70,447; the project guide and Press Backgrounder say 70,446. Say "about 70,000 acres."
5. **The CCC.**
   - Three companies (2767, 2771, 2772) of about 200 men each worked in the badlands from 1934 to 1941. The North Dakota State Historical Society sponsored them.
   - Company 2767 camped on the west bank of the Little Missouri in today's South Unit from July 1934 to 1937.
   - Company 2771 camped near Jones Creek from 1939 to November 1941.
   - Pay was $30 a month, most of it sent home. Enrollees were 17 to 23½.
   - Source: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/civilian-conservation-corps.htm **HIGH**
   - What they built:
     - **South Unit:** "the now-abandoned East Entrance Station, the entrance pylons, and portions of the park's roads and trails."
     - **North Unit:** the River Bend Overlook shelter and the Juniper campground shelters.
     - **Nearby:** structures at the Chateau de Morès.
     - They quarried local sandstone by hand.
     - Source: NPS CCC page. **HIGH**
   - **Not supported by NPS:** the brief lists CCC structures at Painted Canyon and Wind Canyon. No source found. **Unverified — do not say.**
   - CCC veteran quote: "You learned how to live with other men, you learned self esteem ... you learned about yourself." NPS CCC page. **HIGH**
6. **Old East Entrance Station.** It fell out of use when I-94 was built and the entrance road moved. Today it is a 0.8-mile round-trip walk through a prairie dog town. NPS CCC page; NPS South Unit page. **HIGH**
7. **Peaceful Valley Ranch.** Source for all points: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/historyculture/peaceful-valley-ranch.htm **HIGH**
   - It is "the only original ranch house remaining in the South Unit."
   - First settler: Eldridge "Gerry" Paddock in 1883, a market hunter and associate of de Morès. Benjamin Lamb, 22, built the ranch house c. 1885.
   - In 1918 the Olsen family started "one of the first 'dude ranches.'" It was named Peaceful Valley Ranch in 1922.
   - It was sold to the government in 1936 and became RDA headquarters, with CCC, WPA and ERA staff housed there 1934–39.
   - It was park headquarters from 1947 until the Medora visitor center and administration building opened in 1959.
   - Trail rides ran 1918–2014. Three buildings are on the National Register.
   - **No TR connection.** Don't imply one.
   - **CONFLICT:** the project guide says rides are still offered. NPS says rides ended in 2014; the project guide is out of date.
8. **Scenic Loop closure and reopening.**
   - In 2019, severe rain caused slides, and "sections of a 4-mile stretch slid down a steep embankment." Two more miles were later judged unsafe, so drivers had to turn around partway.
   - The loop reopened with a ribbon-cutting on **Tuesday, November 25, 2025**, during the first major snow of the winter.
   - The repaired section runs from the East River Road intersection to the Old East Entrance Station pull-off, "more than six miles of road."
   - Cost: "approximately $51 million" from the Great American Outdoors Act Legacy Restoration Fund, signed August 2020.
   - Sources: NPS news release, Nov. 25, 2025 (https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/news/theodore-roosevelt-national-park-celebrates-completion-of-south-unit-scenic-road-repair-project.htm); North Dakota Monitor, Nov. 25, 2025. **HIGH**
   - Sen. Hoeven compared the work to "almost like building a bridge." ND Monitor. **HIGH**
   - TRPL's own CCMO is quoted in that ND Monitor story.
   - NPS uses "slides." The brief's word "slumping" is also accurate: the NPS Coal Vein guide calls the local process "slumping."
9. **Loop facts.** NPS calls it the "36 Mile Scenic Drive" and says to "plan at least 2 hours." https://www.nps.gov/thro/planyourvisit/south-unit.htm **HIGH**
   - Visitation: 733,000 in 2024; about 100,000 vehicles a year on the South Unit road. NPS release. **HIGH**
10. **Check before recording.** As of May 2026, NPS says Ridgeline Nature Trail is "currently inaccessible due to road collapse." Confirm its status before scripting a stop there. NPS South Unit page. **HIGH** for the statement; status may have changed.

**Anecdote:** NPS's 1928 verdict that "a national park does not seem to be justified" makes a good contrast with today's 700,000-plus visitors a year.

## D. Geology

1. **Formations.** The South Unit exposes the **Bullion Creek Formation**, which is brightly colored ("yellows and tans"). Above it lies the **Sentinel Butte Formation**, in "somber gray to brown colors." Sentinel Butte is about 300 ft thick in the South Unit and is the only one of the two exposed in the North Unit. Source: Hoganson & Campbell, NDGS, "Paleontology of Theodore Roosevelt National Park," https://dmr.nd.gov/dmr/ndgs/TRNP **HIGH**
   - The boundary between them is usually the base of a pink clinker bed called the "HT Butte clinker." Same source. **HIGH**
2. **Age.** Both formations were laid down in the Paleocene, "between about 60-55 million years ago" (NDGS). **HIGH**
   - **CONFLICT on age:**
     - NPS geology page: "over 65 million years ago … Paleocene."
     - NPS Painted Canyon page: "around 65 million years ago."
     - NPS Coal Vein guide: "60-million-year geologic history."
     - The brief's "~55–60 million years" matches NDGS. Use "about 55 to 60 million years ago."
3. **Where the sediment came from.** Sand, silt and mud washed east "off the eastern slopes" of the rising Rocky Mountains. Ash from western volcanoes settled in standing water and became **bentonite**. https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/geologicformations.htm **HIGH**
4. **Ancient swamp.** The area was "a flat, swampy area" on the edge of a broad delta, with sequoia, bald cypress and magnolia. NPS geologic formations page. **HIGH**
   - *Champsosaurus*, a 10-ft crocodile-like reptile from about 55 million years ago, was excavated in the South Unit in October 1995. NPS fossils page. **HIGH**
   - Fossil stumps up to 7–8 ft across stand in growth position on the Petrified Forest Plateau. NDGS; NPS fossils page. **HIGH**
5. **Glaciers turned the river.**
   - The ancestral Little Missouri flowed north toward Hudson Bay.
   - In the Pleistocene ice ages, which began about 2 million years ago, ice sheets reached the North Unit's northern edge. They blocked the north-flowing rivers and forced them east and south into the Missouri–Mississippi system.
   - The steeper new course made the Little Missouri cut deep, carving the badlands.
   - Granite glacial erratics mark the ice's southern limit in the North Unit.
   - Sources: NPS geologic formations, glaciers and Painted Canyon pages. **HIGH**
   - Note: NDGS does not discuss the diversion; this rests on NPS.
6. **Lignite.** Peat from fallen swamp vegetation compressed into lignite, a "soft, woody-textured coal." TR and his men "shoveled lignite coal from the hillsides" for their stove. NPS geologic formations page. **HIGH**
   - TR, 1885: "if there is a good coal vein, as on my ranch, the coal must be dug out and hauled in." HTR ch. I. **HIGH**
7. **Clinker ("scoria").** Lightning, prairie fires or spontaneous combustion ignite coal seams. The burning seam bakes the rock above "into a hard, natural brick that geologists call clinker, but is locally called 'scoria.'" The red color comes from oxidized iron. The hard clinker caps buttes. Sources: NPS geologic formations page; NPS Coal Vein guide; Bluemle, NDGS "North Dakota's Clinker." **HIGH**
   - True "scoria" is volcanic, and TR himself thought some buttes were "of volcanic origin." He was wrong. Script can note the misnomer.
8. **The Coal Vein.** Source: NPS Coal Vein trail guide, https://www.nps.gov/thro/planyourvisit/upload/Coal-Vein-508.pdf **HIGH** for all points.
   - A 12-ft-thick seam caught fire in 1951 (lightning is named at post 11) and burned 26 years, 1951–1977.
   - The ground collapsed as the coal burned away. "Before the fire, the land was level with the top of the stairs."
   - "Visitors could see smoke, glowing coals, and sometimes flames. They even roasted marshmallows over the fire!"
   - Bluemle (NDGS) places it "at Buck Hill." **HIGH**
9. **Bentonite.** It "can absorb up to five times its weight in water," "looks like popcorn when dry," and becomes "sticky, slick mud when wet." NPS Coal Vein guide. **HIGH**
   - Trails can stay slippery "for days." NPS safety page. **HIGH**
10. **Slumping.** Water soaks into cracks and saturates bentonite layers, and hillsides slide slowly. It happens "on a very large scale when entire hillsides slide." NPS Coal Vein guide, post 13. **HIGH**
    - This connects to the 2019 road failure. Saying the road damage came from the same process is a reasonable inference; NPS only says "slides."
11. **North vs. south slopes and erosion.** North faces get less sun and hold moisture, so juniper grows there. "Juniper woodlands on the north faces of buttes also slow the effect of erosion, and the north faces of the hills are not as steep as the south faces." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/forests.htm **HIGH**
12. **Caprocks.** Harder sandstone or clinker "acts like a helmet" over softer rock; this is differential erosion. Coal Vein guide; NPS Painted Canyon page. **HIGH**
13. **Climate.** About 15 inches of precipitation a year, mostly in "heavy, erosive downpours." The guide adds that the dry climate "keeps the badlands from eroding away more quickly." NPS geology page; Coal Vein guide. **HIGH**
14. **Wind Canyon.** NPS calls it "wind-carved" and "a great example of the ongoing erosion." It "channels the wind." Hollows in the walls are nest sites for great horned owls. Short trail; NPS gives 0.4 mi. https://www.nps.gov/places/wind-canyon.htm **HIGH**
    - It is one of the best places to scan for elk at sunrise or sunset. NPS elk page. **HIGH**
15. **Buck Hill.** One of the highest points in the park, with a 360° view. Ripple marks in the rock show "this high point used to be the bottom of an ancient streambed." https://www.nps.gov/places/buck-hill.htm **HIGH**
16. **Concretions.** The famous Cannonball Concretions pullout is in the **North Unit**, not on the South Unit loop (project guide; NPS Geodiversity Atlas caption). **MED**
    - No NPS text describing concretions on the South Unit loop was found. Keep any mention general.
17. **Petrified wood.** Silica from volcanic ash replaced the wood, sometimes preserving growth rings. NPS geologic formations page. **HIGH**
    - **Unverified:** the project guide's "3rd largest petrified forest in the U.S." No NPS or NDGS source found. Don't say it.
18. **Erosion rate.** **No TRNP-specific figure found.** The "about an inch a year" figure some guides cite belongs to Badlands National Park in South Dakota. **Do not use a rate.**

**Anecdotes**
- Families roasting marshmallows over a burning coal seam in the 1950s–70s (Coal Vein guide).
- TR's ghost: one winter night, riding home, his party rounded a ravine and "saw in our path a tall white column of smoke rising from a rift in the snowy crags ahead of us … we were for a moment almost as startled as if we had seen a ghost." A new burning mine had opened half a mile from the ranch house. RL ch. V. **HIGH**

## E. Wildlife

### Distance rule (read first)

- **TRNP says 25 yards for all wildlife, bison included.**
  - "Maintain at least 25 yards distance from all wildlife." https://www.nps.gov/thro/planyourvisit/wildlife-viewing.htm
  - "It's recommended to keep at least 25 yards between you and bison." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/bison-buffalo.htm
  - The NPS trail-safety audio says to give a bison or horse "at least 25 yards," using the "rule of thumb": if your thumb at arm's length covers the animal, you are far enough away.
  - **HIGH**
- **CONFLICT:** the project's TRNP guide says 100 yards for bison and elk. That matches Yellowstone's rule for bears and wolves, not TRNP's published rule. **Script 25 yards**, and add that more is better.
  - "If an animal moves away from you as you approach, you are too close!" NPS wildlife viewing page. **HIGH**
- In the car: "stay in your vehicle, and observe them quietly." Bison "may attack without warning." Feeding any animal is prohibited. NPS bison and safety pages. **HIGH**
- Bison injury statistics specific to TRNP were not found. Don't cite any.

### Bison

1. **Pre-park.** There were none when the park was founded in 1947. In 1956, 29 bison arrived from Fort Niobrara National Wildlife Refuge in Nebraska: 24 females and 5 males. Sources: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/bison-management.htm; NPS bison page. **HIGH**
   - The brief's question mark is resolved: it was Fort Niobrara.
   - By 1962 the herd numbered 145, and 20 (10 bulls, 10 cows) were moved to found the North Unit herd. **HIGH**
2. **Herd targets.** **CONFLICT:** the bison management page says 200–500 in the South Unit and 100–300 in the North Unit; the bison natural-history page says 200–400 and 100–300. Say "a few hundred."
3. **Roundups.** Every 2–3 years, in October, after the rut. A helicopter pushes the herd to corrals. Between 1956 and 2016 the park shipped out 3,752 bison, 92% of them to tribes; recipients include 67 tribes and reservations. NPS bison management. **HIGH**
4. **Size and speed.**
   - Bulls weigh up to 2,000 lb and stand 6 ft or more at the shoulder. Cows weigh up to 1,100 lb.
   - Bison "can run up to 35 miles per hour, jump 6 feet vertically, and pivot quickly."
   - "If a bison raises its tail, especially if the tail is straight up in the air, a charge may be imminent."
   - Source: NPS bison page. **HIGH**
5. **Behavior.**
   - Bison like prairie dog towns for fresh grass shoots and loose soil to **wallow** in.
   - The rut "typically peaks in July," and a prime bull can lose up to 200 lb.
   - Calves are orange-red and "up and moving within 3 hours of birth."
   - Source: NPS bison page. **HIGH**
6. **Near extinction.** "By the turn of the twentieth century, the thundering herds of millions were reduced to approximately 300 bison." NPS bison page. **HIGH** (agency figure; estimates vary widely)

### Horses

TRPL is supportive of the horses (TRPL staff direction). Keep this section factual and warm, and don't editorialize against NPS.

7. **Origin.** NPS calls them "Originally descended from domestic stock" and says they are "maintained in the park today as a cultural resource." NPS wildlife viewing page. **HIGH**
   - Researcher Castle McLaughlin traces them to horses from Native American tribes and local ranches, plus domestic stallions added from the late 1970s through the 1990s. AP via CBS News, March 2024. **MED**
   - Forum/Grand Forks Herald: ancestry includes ponies surrendered by Sitting Bull's followers at Fort Buford in 1881. **MED** (single secondary source; attribute it and get a second source before scripting)
   - McLaughlin: "They really are sort of living history." AP via CBS. **HIGH** (quote as reported)
8. **Fenced in.** The horses' ancestors were "accidentally fenced into the park in the 1950s." AP via CBS News. **MED**
9. **Policy shifts.** Until the early 1970s official policy was to remove all the horses. It was then reversed to keep a demonstration herd depicting the "historic scene." Grand Forks Herald. **MED**
10. **2022–2024 controversy.**
    - The park's Livestock Plan environmental assessment, begun in 2022, preferred removing the horses (and covered about nine North Unit longhorns).
    - Public comments heavily favored keeping them.
    - Congress's Interior appropriations report "strongly recommends" keeping the horses (Sen. Hoeven, March 2024).
    - On April 25, 2024, Hoeven announced NPS had committed to keep a herd. His statement: "This will allow for a healthy herd of wild horses to be maintained at the park."
    - Sources: AP (CBS; ABC); Grand Forks Herald. **HIGH**
    - Gov. Burgum had offered state help (NDSU, Dickinson State expertise). **HIGH**
    - In April 2025 Hoeven introduced a bill requiring at least 150 horses. ND Monitor. **MED** (bill status not checked)
11. **Numbers.**
    - The 2024 roundup (Oct. 13–17) brought 179 horses into the corrals; none were removed. Horses were microchipped and 15 GPS-collared. https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/horses.htm **HIGH**
    - In 2026, NPS describes the collared mares' groups as representing "approximately 137 out of 198 (70%) horses in the park." Same page, April 20, 2026 update. **HIGH**
    - **Script safely:** "about 200 horses, all in the South Unit."
    - The 1978 assessment still sets a 35–60 herd cap (Supt. Richman, via AP). **MED**; sensitive, so mention only if needed.
12. **Where to see them.** Groups are often seen from Painted Canyon, Buck Hill and Wind Canyon; collar data show heavy use of the southeast corner and the area north of Boicourt. NPS horses page. **HIGH**
13. **Vocabulary.** NPS uses "feral horses"; advocates and TRPL say "wild horses." Use "wild horses" and mention once that NPS manages them as a cultural resource.

### Prairie dogs

14. **Species.** Only the black-tailed prairie dog lives in the park. They weigh 1–3 lb and are 14–17 in. long. They are rodents in the squirrel family. French explorers called them "petits chiens."
    - The basic family unit is the "coterie," and members greet with an "identifying kiss or sniff."
    - They do not hibernate.
    - Source: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/prairie-dogs.htm **HIGH**
15. **Alarm calls.** Their "barks … sound more like high-pitched squeaks," with "distinctive vocalization patterns" for different threats, and an "all clear" once danger passes. NPS. **HIGH**
16. **Jump-yip.** A prairie dog stretches upward and calls, and the display ripples across the town like a stadium wave. Researchers found it is contagious and lets an individual gauge how alert its neighbors are. Hare et al., "Catch the wave," *Proc. R. Soc. B* 281 (2014): 20132153, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3896008 **HIGH**
    - The study was not done in TRNP. Present it as general black-tailed prairie dog behavior.
17. **Ecosystem role.** Grazing favors forbs, which draws bison and pronghorn. Badgers, coyotes, foxes, bobcats, snakes and raptors hunt them. NPS prairie dog page. **HIGH**
    - "Keystone species" is common usage among biologists but is **not** on the TRNP page. Attribute it ("biologists often call them a keystone species"). **MED**
18. **Scale then and now.** "In 1901, scientists surveyed a single prairie dog town that covered an area of 25,000 square miles and contained an estimated 400,000,000 prairie dogs." NPS. **HIGH** (attribute to NPS)
    - Poisoning campaigns nearly wiped out prairie dogs and the black-footed ferret. NPS. **HIGH**
    - "The park road passes through three large prairie dog towns in the South Unit." NPS wildlife viewing page. **HIGH**
19. **Cautions.** Don't feed them. They bite, and their fleas can carry plague. NPS. **HIGH**

### Other species

20. **Elk.**
    - Gone from the region "by the late 1800s." Reintroduced to the South Unit on **March 13, 1985**, from Wind Cave National Park, S.D.
    - The target is 100–400 animals. The herd passed 1,000 by about 2010, and the park now does annual reductions with volunteers.
    - Best seen at dawn or dusk from Wind Canyon or the Buck Hill area. Bulls bugle in the September rut.
    - Sources: https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/elk.htm; NPS elk management page. **HIGH**
    - The number of founding elk was not found. Don't cite one.
21. **Pronghorn.**
    - They are not true antelope; they are the last surviving member of family Antilocapridae.
    - Speed up to 55 mph, making them "second-fastest land mammal in the world, behind the cheetah."
    - About 320° field of view; they can spot movement 3 miles away.
    - They are the only animal that sheds its horns (the sheaths) every year.
    - Look at the Beef Corral prairie dog town and Lindbo Flats north of Boicourt. Pronghorn enter the park only "occasionally."
    - Sources: NPS pronghorn and wildlife viewing pages. **HIGH**
    - **Wording caution:** "fastest in North America" and "second-fastest in the world" are both true. Avoid "fastest land animal."
22. **Mule deer vs. white-tailed deer.**
    - Mule deer are more common. They have big ears and a thin, black-tipped tail, and their antler tines fork into a "Y."
    - When startled they "stot," bounding stiff-legged with all four feet hitting together. Several groups live around park headquarters.
    - White-tails keep to the river bottoms and wooded draws, and raise a white "flag" tail when alarmed.
    - Sources: NPS mule deer, white-tailed deer and wildlife viewing pages. **HIGH**
23. **Coyotes.** "The most commonly spotted predator in Theodore Roosevelt National Park." Look in prairie dog towns at twilight, and "listen for coyotes howling at dusk year-round." NPS coyote and mammals pages. **HIGH**
24. **Missing predators.** Wolves and grizzly bears were driven out in the 1800s and never returned. That is why the park must actively manage bison, elk and horses. NPS bison management and mammals pages. **HIGH**
25. **Birds.**
    - "More than 185" species have been recorded. Year-round residents include golden eagles, wild turkeys and great horned owls.
    - Watch for golden eagles over the Little Missouri floodplain.
    - Sources: NPS birds and wildlife viewing pages. **HIGH**
    - The western meadowlark became North Dakota's state bird on March 22, 1947. Prairie Public, Dakota Datebook, Mar. 22, 2024. **MED** (confirm with the ND Century Code if scripted)
    - Sharp-tailed grouse are listed in the project guide only, not on NPS pages checked. **MED**
    - TR's own birds:
      - "One of our sweetest, loudest songsters is the meadow-lark." HTR ch. I.
      - TR called the "Missouri skylark" (today's Sprague's pipit) "the prince of them all." HTR ch. I.
      - The skylark identification is modern interpretation. **MED**
26. **Prairie rattlesnake.** The park's only venomous reptile; NPS says "they are not as common as they were in Theodore Roosevelt's time." Bullsnakes and racers are seen more often, often on the road. Rattlesnakes and black widows often live in prairie dog burrows. NPS reptiles, wildlife viewing and safety pages. **HIGH**
27. **Porcupines.** 10–30 lb. Look high in cottonwoods along the river, easiest in fall and winter when the leaves are down. They are often seen beside the road at night. A baby is a "porcupette." NPS porcupine page. **HIGH**
28. **Badgers.** Solitary and mostly nocturnal; they dig out prairie dogs. "Coyotes can sometimes be observed waiting nearby to catch rodents fleeing." Look in prairie dog towns at sunrise, including at the Old East Entrance Station and Lindbo Flats. NPS badger page. **HIGH**
    - TR, 1885: "A badger is a most desperate fighter, and an overmatch for a coyote." HTR ch. VI. **HIGH**

**Anecdote:** a badger and a coyote working the same prairie dog town (NPS badger page). TR noted the same pairing in 1885: "Around the prairie-dog towns it is always well to keep a look-out for the smaller carnivora, especially coyotes and badgers." HTR ch. VI.

## F. Plants and landscape

1. **Plant count.** "Over 400 species of plants have been identified within the park. As many as 500 species … may inhabit the park." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/plants.htm **HIGH**
2. **Wildflower calendar.** The pasqueflower blooms first, in April. Peak bloom is June–July. Sunflowers, asters and rabbitbrush last into August–September. "In late September … cottonwood leaves turn a brilliant gold." NPS plants page. **HIGH**
3. **North vs. south slopes.** "Rocky Mountain juniper woodlands are the most common forest type in the park because the junipers benefit from a microclimate created on the north face of the buttes." South faces are "hotter and drier." Juniper berries feed Townsend's solitaires, waxwings and robins. NPS forests page. **HIGH**
   - The Coal Vein guide adds that north slopes "receive very little direct sunlight which helps retain moisture." **HIGH**
4. **Cottonwood bottoms.** Periodic flooding "has helped trees such as the cottonwood to become established on the floodplain." Green ash, American elm and box elder grow there too. The bottoms shelter white-tailed deer, porcupines, beavers, orioles and owls. NPS floodplains and forests pages. **HIGH**
5. **Grasses.** Grassland is "the most abundant habitat type" in the park. Species include western wheatgrass, needle-and-thread, little bluestem and saltgrass. Sagebrush and wild rose creep in over time. "Periodic fire, whether natural or prescribed … is necessary." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/prairies.htm **HIGH**
6. **The river.** The Little Missouri varies "from a muddy trickle to a raging flood to a frozen highway." It is the only water in the park that supports fish. NPS rivers page. **HIGH**
   - TR's matching image: in winter the river "lay in its bed fixed and immovable as a bar of bent steel, and then at night wolves and lynxes traveled up and down it as if it had been a highway passing in front of the ranch house." AUTO. **HIGH**
7. **Springs.** The badlands' springs are often "muddy holes in the ground often full of foul-tasting alkaline water." NPS springs page. **HIGH**
   - TR, Sept. 8, 1883, to Alice: the water is "so alkaline that the very cow's milk tastes of it." TRC. **MED** (TRC transcription, not checked against the page image)
8. **No picking.** Collecting plants, rocks or fossils is prohibited. NPS. **HIGH**

## G. Night sky and seasons (brief)

1. **Dark skies.** The park "is over 30 miles from the nearest large city." On clear nights you can see the Milky Way, planets and the Andromeda Galaxy without a telescope. "Occasionally, the Northern Lights … may be visible." https://www.nps.gov/thro/learn/nature/lightscape.htm **HIGH**
   - Threats include oil-and-gas flaring and light from Medora, Belfield and Watford City. Same page. **HIGH**
   - Buck Hill is good for stargazing. NPS Buck Hill page. **HIGH**
2. **TR saw the aurora here.** "Under the ceaseless, shifting play of the Northern Lights, or lighted only by the wintry brilliance of the stars, the snow-clad plains stretch out into dead and endless wastes of glimmering white." RL ch. V "Winter Weather." **HIGH**
   - TRPL has an Aurora Watch tool (aurora.labs.trlibrary.com) for an optional cross-promotion.
3. **Seasons.** Spring wildflowers peak in June–July. Bison rut peaks in July, the elk rut runs in September, cottonwoods turn gold in late September. Winter can close parts of the road. NPS plants, bison and elk pages; project guide. **HIGH** / MED
4. **Climate.** About 15 inches of precipitation a year. Summers can top 100°F and winters can fall below −30°F. Project guide; Press Backgrounder. **MED**

## I. TR's voice recordings

1. **What exists.** TR recorded cylinders for Thomas A. Edison in **August 1912**, during the Progressive ("Bull Moose") campaign. Library of Congress records (the originals are not held by LOC; preservation masters were made by the Theodore Roosevelt Association in 1982):
   - "Progressive Covenant with the People," 3 min. 13 sec. (LCCN 99391565)
   - "The Right of the People to Rule," 4 min. (LCCN 99391599)
   - **HIGH**
2. **Other 1912 titles** in the Vincent Voice Library (MSU) and Internet Archive:
   - "The Farmer and the Business Man"
   - "Why the Trusts and Bosses Oppose the Progressive Party"
   - "The Liberty of the People"
   - "Social and Industrial Justice"
   - Internet Archive item `theoroos1912` ("Edison presidential recordings from the campaign of 1912") has "The liberty of the people" and "Social and industrial justice," total run time 7:26, marked **Public Domain**: https://archive.org/details/theoroos1912
   - **HIGH**
   - Wikimedia Commons holds "Social and industrial justice-1912 speech by Theodore Roosevelt.mp3," "The Right of the People to Rule (Speech by Theodore Roosevelt).wav," and "Theodore Roosevelt 'The liberty of the people' speech.ogg." **HIGH**
3. **Other recordings.**
   - 1898 cylinders on which TR announces the bugle calls at San Juan Hill (MSU Vincent Voice Library catalog). **MED**
   - A 1913 address to the Boys Progressive League, dated March 4, 1913 per Wikisource (citing Allen Koenigsberg); audio is on Wikimedia Commons as "Roosevelt - Address to the Boys Progressive League.ogg." **MED** for the date.
4. **Copyright.** U.S. sound recordings published before 1923 entered the public domain on Jan. 1, 2022, under the Music Modernization Act. LOC's item pages still say users must make their own rights assessment and credit "Library of Congress, Motion Picture, Broadcasting, and Recorded Sound Division." **HIGH** (law)
   - **Action:** confirm the source file you use is a transfer of the 1912 Edison cylinder, not a newer restoration with its own rights claim.
5. **Partisanship problem.** **Every known TR recording is political.** The 1912 discs are Progressive Party campaign speeches. "The Right of the People to Rule" attacks "My opponents" and the "Bourbon reactionaries." The 1913 boys' address urges listeners to "join the Progressive Party." **None is about conservation or the Badlands.** **HIGH**
6. **Least partisan passages.** Candidate excerpts are below. Transcribe from the actual audio before scripting, because the Wikisource texts are drawn from printed speech versions.
   - (a) The close of "The Right of the People to Rule" (Wikisource text, from the Carnegie Hall speech of March 12, 1912): "In the long fight for righteousness the watchword for all of us is spend and be spent." The MSU catalog confirms the recording ends on the watchword "spend and be spent." It is about 4 seconds and about character, but the speech's context is a campaign.
   - (b) The end of the 1913 boys' address (Wikisource transcript): "...don't flinch, don't foul, and hit the line hard." It is about 3 seconds and about sportsmanship and character, but the sentence before it names the Progressive Party, so use only the final clause, as a sound bite.
   - **Recommendation:** use at most one of these as a brief "this is his actual voice" moment, framed as "Here is Roosevelt's real voice, recorded in 1912." Don't name the campaign, and say plainly that recordings of him are rare.
